# GovWell

## Summary

GovWell replaces the permitting, licensing, planning and code-enforcement system of record in small US cities and counties, displacing paper, ERP add-on modules, small-vendor permitting tools and, at least once, Accela [1][2][3]. It sells a cloud platform that its own staff configure and launch in weeks (3 to 11 in its published examples), priced per module, with AI features shipping by mid-2025 [4][5][6]. Founded in April 2023, it reports more than 200 agencies in 40+ states and has raised $34.5 million, including a $25 million Series A led by Insight Partners in May 2026 [7][8][9]. The lesson: it won on deployment speed, service and council-vote procurement before AI led its marketing, and that playbook is untested in RFP-driven cities above about 100,000 people (inferred) [10][11][12].

## The legacy it attacks

The enterprise tier is old and consolidated. Accela had 300 client cities when it agreed to buy Tidemark, a vendor founded in 1984, in April 2001 [13]. Infor agreed to buy Hansen and its roughly 470 customers in May 2007 [14]. Tyler bought EnerGov less than two years before September 2014 [15]. More than 60 agencies chose it in that span, including Kansas City for nearly $6 million [15].

GovWell mostly fights the tier below. Indiana shows the tiers. Accela runs the Indianapolis and Fort Wayne portals, and EnerGov runs Carmel and Crown Point [16]. iWorQ and CitizenServe serve small cities, GovWell runs Plymouth, Harrison County and LaPorte County, and many rural counties still take paper [16]. GovWell says 80% of its sales directly replace a legacy vendor [9]. At least once that vendor was Accela: Paradise, California moved permits and inspections off Accela Citizen Access to GovWell in June 2025 [3][17].

Operators are small: in 2014 the typical code official worked in a one- to nine-person department serving under 75,000 residents [18].

These systems stick for five reasons.

- **Integration.** Branson, Missouri ran its CentralSquare Naviline ERP on an AS/400 with DB2 for 20 years, while permitting and code enforcement ran in a separate CitizenServe product that any new ERP had to interface with [19].
- **Failure risk.** California's BreEZe licensing system grew from $28 million to $96 million while covering about half its planned regulatory entities [20]. Macon County, North Carolina spent nearly a year on a replacement that never went live [21].
- **Contracts.** Three-year terms, auto-renewal and bans on like-for-like replacement during the term are standard (inferred). GovWell's own order forms carry all three [5].
- **Data.** Under GovWell's terms, the buyer must extract records from the old system, or chase the old vendor for them [22].
- **Fatigue.** A Building Code Forum veteran of 20-plus years wrote in March 2026 that paper costs about $1,000 a year. He set that against at least five systems he had been part of, each costing hundreds of thousands of dollars [23].

## Origin

Ben Cohen grew up helping his father, a contractor, file permits; as an Uber engineer he faxed permit papers at night [24]. He studied computer science at Princeton [25]. Troy LeCaire studied government at Cornell, worked for two US senators from Wisconsin, and was the first employee at RippleMatch, which grew to $10 million in annual revenue [25]. They met at a founder speed-dating event at Fractal Software, a New York incubator [24][26]. GovWell started in April 2023 [7]. Bienville Capital put in $1 million that month [27].

The insight came from calling hundreds of municipalities about their software. LeCaire said users "didn't like it despite paying thousands if not millions for it" [24]. They signed five governments before writing code [24]. The May 2023 site pitched configurable, affordable software with free 24/7 support for smaller cities priced out of expensive systems [28].

Why then? Four forces.

1. **Cost of building.** GovWell argues that AI lets a small engineering team build deeply configurable software that once needed armies of implementation consultants [29].
2. **Incumbent drift.** Work-Bench, its seed lead, says legacy implementations run 12 to 18 months, cost multiples of the software, and price out small and mid-size towns [30]. Consolidation gave incumbents new owners: Cox agreed to pay $1.8 billion for OpenGov in 2024, Tyler took MyGov and Granicus took SmartGov [31][32].
3. **Labor.** More than 80% of code officials planned to retire within 15 years of 2014 [18].
4. **Law.** States now impose permit clocks. Indiana lets applicants use private reviewers when deadlines slip, from January 2026 [33]. Florida added a five-business-day review category for some residential permits from July 2026 [34]. Tennessee lets applications be deemed approved when review deadlines are missed, from January 2027 [35]. These laws postdate the founding, so they are accelerants, not the trigger (inferred).

## First wedge

The first job was building permits [24]: intake, plan review, inspections and certificates, the most frequent public transaction in a small town (inferred). By April 2024 the product also covered planning, code enforcement, licenses, public works and fire, but permits and plan review led the pitch [36].

The first customer was the small town. The June 2024 price list had three tiers: up to 10,000 residents, 10,000 to 25,000, and 25,000 to 50,000, starting at $5,000 a year [37]. The April 2024 logo strip showed Hampton, Georgia (9,400 people); Ridgefield, Washington; Decatur, Texas; Champlain, New York; Kingsland, Georgia; and Hutto, Texas (42,700) [36][8].

Why this segment:

- **Procurement is a vote.** Fairview, Tennessee approved its contract at a board meeting after a work-session demo, with no RFP mentioned in the minutes [2].
- **Permit fees pay for it.** Manor, Texas said its permit technology fees, over $30,000 a year, roughly cover a $34,000 contract [38].
- **Enterprise vendors price for bigger cities.** GovWell's guide calls EnerGov extremely expensive and lengthy to implement, and says small jurisdictions can get deprioritized (vendor claim) [32].

## First 10 customers

GovWell has not published its first ten. Public records show the method.

- **Found.** The founders say they talked with more than 250 agencies [39].
- **Sold.** Early contracts bundled implementation, data migration, unlimited users and free support, with a one-year minimum and many three-to-five-year signings [37]. The site promised a 10-minute support response [36].
- **Implemented.** Each customer got a dedicated account manager who ran implementation and training [37]. Employee one was a deployment strategist; employee two was a business developer [25].

The median sales cycle is three months [9]. Fairview approved on February 6, 2025 and planned about 90 days to implement [2]. Manor spoke with several providers, all priced between $31,000 and $34,000, before choosing GovWell [38].

Pricing is per module and scales with population [37]. Willow Park, Texas faced list prices of $10,000 a year for building permits, $8,000 for code enforcement, $7,000 for planning and $5,000 for business licenses [5]. Discounts are tied to signing deadlines. Manor's $26,500 in setup fees were waived if it signed by November 14, 2025 [40]. Willow Park's year-one cost fell from $49,000 to $40,000 if signed by June 13, 2026, via a discount the form attributes to GovWell's CEO [5].

## What made it hard

**Procurement above the small-town band.** In 2014, 95% of the new contracts open to EnerGov involved competitive RFPs [15]. Winston-Salem's 2026 permitting RFP asks for five references from places near 400,000 people [12]. La Porte County, the largest population GovWell cites in its testimonials, issues permits only for unincorporated areas [4][49]. The largest jurisdiction found is Medina County, Ohio (182,470 people in 2020), which authorized a GovWell agreement by resolution in June 2026, with no RFP visible [57][65]. GovWell has hired a capture and proposal manager [25]. Its RFP toolkit tells buyers to weigh recent, relevant implementations over years in business and headcount [50]. It also sells through reseller Carahsoft, which invoices Casa Grande [22]. State rules add paperwork, such as Arizona's E-Verify, Israel-boycott and forced-labor certifications in that contract [22]. Budgets still set timing: Willow Park's first subscription invoice falls on October 1, 2026 [5].

**Data migration.** The customer exports data from the legacy system, or gets it from the old vendor [22]. Migration runs after go-live, excludes data cleaning and paper digitization, and extra migration hours bill at $200 [22]. Cutovers can be partial. McCall, Idaho keeps existing permits in its old system while new applications go to GovWell [51]. Woodstock still links an iWorQ portal for zoning-violation reports [47].

**Service cost.** Speed is bought with people. Casa Grande's order included 148 deployment hours, 129 migration hours and 18 training sessions [22]. 21 of 71 staff work in deployment and support [25]. No gross-margin figure is public.

**Incumbent response.** OpenGov added AI pre-check, intake review and plan review in April 2026 [52]. Accela bought Civira AI in July 2026 to speed configuration and deployments [53]. Both target GovWell's selling points, AI and fast launches (inferred).

**Talent.** Staff work from a New York office three to five days a week [54]. Deployment bios point to campaigns, policy and startups more than building departments (inferred) [25].

**Product gaps.** Two 2025 reviewers flagged code-enforcement mailers without photos, no way to reassign inspectors, and weak search [55].

**Cash.** At the seed, LeCaire said GovWell was on a path to positive cash flow [24]. It has since hired heavily into go-to-market roles, and its burn is not public [25].

## Strategy

GovWell is greenfield: a new cloud system of record for permitting and licensing, not an overlay [56]. Delivery is migration-led and service-heavy: a deployment strategist configures the system and a data team migrates records [4]. GovWell markets configuration updates as free forever, but its order forms count them against 20 to 40 service hours a year [4][5][22]. No acquisitions were found, and it sells software, not an AI-run service (inferred).

The path from wedge to core:

1. **Land building permits in a small town** [24][37].
2. **Add modules.** Casa Grande bought seven, from building permits to fire inspections and public works, for $135,638 a year plus $95,878 in setup [22].
3. **Add departments.** Medina County authorized it for building, sanitary engineering, highway engineering and stormwater [57].
4. **Sell AI as an upsell.** The Community Assistant is free up to 100 questions a month, then an upgrade [22]. Willow Park got the premium tier free for a year [5]. AutoCheck drafts review comments that staff confirm [58].
5. **Build applicant networks and own the payment rail.** Contractors already on GovWell in nearby Ohio towns reused their logins in Twinsburg [45]. Payments run only through its integrated processor, Finix [22].
6. **Move beyond community development** into public works, benefits and finance, then statewide systems, unemployment benefits and visas [44][29].

Steps 1 to 5 are visible. Step 6 is not. GovWell sits beside the ERP rather than replacing it [56]. AI marketing also came late. AI application summaries shipped in June 2025 [6], yet the homepage did not mention AI as late as December 2025 [10]. The rebrand as an AI operating system came in February 2026, when GovWell already served 100+ agencies [11].

## What others can copy

1. **Enter where procurement is a vote.** Price modules from $5,000 a year and show how permit fees cover the bill [37][38]. Test: share of deals closed without an RFP.
2. **Productize deployment.** Publish go-live times of 3 to 11 weeks and cap contract hours, from 60 to 148 [4][40][22]. Go live first, migrate history after [22]. Test: weeks from signature to first permit issued.
3. **Win on service, then add AI.** GovWell had 100+ agencies before AI led its homepage [11][10]. Test: AI attach rate on new deals.
4. **Write retention into the contract, then earn it.** Use three-year terms, auto-renewal, a 5% uplift after the term, and a bar on like-for-like replacement [5]. Test: gross revenue retention at first renewal of three-year terms, which begins in 2026 for 2023 signings and covers most of the base from 2027 to 2029 (inferred).
5. **Build cross-jurisdiction applicant networks.** Shared contractor accounts cut friction in the next town [45]. Test: share of applicants in a new town who already hold an account.
6. **Climb by population band and staff for procurement early.** The top price tier went from 25,000 to 50,000 residents (June 2024) to 25,000+ (December 2024); proposal staff followed [37][59][25]. Test: share of new contract value from places above 50,000 people.

## Open questions

- **Can it pass mid-size RFPs?** The largest public order with a disclosed price is about $136,000 a year from Casa Grande, a city of 53,658 in 2020 [22][60]. Medina County is larger but bought by resolution [57]. Winston-Salem wants references near 400,000 people and every listed integration in the base bid [12].
- **Does the 80% displacement figure include enterprise suites?** Paradise left Accela [3]. Other visible switches came from IDT, iWorQ, ESO Fire RMS, or mixed manual and electronic tools [2][55][48].
- **Will retention hold at first renewal?** Early contracts had a one-year minimum, but current initial terms run three to five years [37][22][40][2]. The claim moved from no churn in May to a 98% renewal rate in October 2026 [9][8]. Ridgefield, Washington left the logo strip between April and July 2024 and now takes permits on SmartGov [36][61][62]. Whether it was ever live is unknown.
- **Is AI a moat?** OpenGov and Accela now ship AI review and AI-assisted deployment [52][53]. GovWell itself tells buyers to make vendors prove AI claims on real scenarios [63].
- **Will private review shrink the agency's job?** Indiana and Florida widened private-provider routes [33][34]. Insight Partners also backs GreenLite, which sells private plan review [64].
- **What are the unit economics?** Revenue, gross margin and services cost are undisclosed [42][9].
